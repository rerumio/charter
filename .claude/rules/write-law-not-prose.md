# Write law, not prose — statements only, never persuasion

A clause that argues for itself invites argument. The moment a text explains why it is fair,
offers an example, or reaches for a metaphor, it stops being something to obey and becomes
something to interpret — and every reader interprets differently. Warmth in a founding document
reads as weakness the first time someone tests it.

- **State, never justify.** A clause says what holds. It never says why it was chosen, what it
  guards against, or what would happen otherwise. "Published text is editable for 7 days and no
  longer" — not "so that no one can win an argument retroactively".
- **No allowances, no illustrations.** No "this permits X and forbids Y", no examples, no "for
  instance", no parenthetical softening. A clause that needs an example to be understood is a
  badly written clause; rewrite the clause.
- **Short sentences, present tense, no hedging.** Never "should", "we try to", "generally",
  "where possible". Formal weight does not mean heavy vocabulary: write "removed", not "subject
  to a removal procedure".
- **Name the subject, never address the reader.** "An author may take their texts and leave" —
  not "you can export your stuff". No second person, no reassurance, no encouragement.
- **The exception.** Files describing the repository rather than governing the platform — the
  README, working notes — are ordinary prose, and the project's plain-speech rule governs them
  instead. This register binds what rules the platform, not what explains the folder.
